# Amazon Leadership Principles — Behavioral Interview Prep

Source: [amazon.jobs — Our Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles)
(16 principles, official text quoted verbatim below)

These aren't just "culture posters" — they're the actual rubric. Every interviewer
in an Amazon loop is assigned **2-3 specific Leadership Principles (LPs)** to probe,
and they score your answer against that LP explicitly, in a shared debrief afterward.
This applies inside the coding and design rounds too, not just a dedicated
"behavioral round" — see the note in
[`../Nailing_DSA/DP_Question_List.md`](../Nailing_DSA/DP_Question_List.md) and
[`../Nailing HLD at Amazon/HLD_Question_List.md`](../Nailing%20HLD%20at%20Amazon/HLD_Question_List.md)
about LPs being woven directly into the technical rounds — a technically perfect
answer paired with a weak LP story can still sink a round.

---

## How to actually use this document

1. **Prepare 8-12 STAR stories from your own career**, not 16 — most real stories
   naturally cover 1-2 LPs each, so a well-chosen story set covers the full list
   without forcing 16 separate anecdotes.
2. **Prioritize these LPs first** if you're short on time — they come up the most
   across reported loops: **Customer Obsession, Ownership, Dive Deep, Bias for
   Action, Earn Trust, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results.**
3. **Use the STAR structure, and weight it like this**:
   - **S**ituation — ~10%
   - **T**ask — ~10%
   - **A**ction — ~50% (the bulk of the answer — and every sentence here should be
     "I," not "we." Amazon wants to know what *you* did.)
   - **R**esult — ~30% (quantify it — a number, a percentage, a dollar amount, a
     time saved. Vague, unquantified outcomes are the single most common reason a
     strong-sounding story scores poorly.)
4. **Close with a brief "what I'd do differently."** It costs one sentence and
   signals self-awareness and continuous learning — which is itself an LP
   (*Learn and Be Curious*).
5. **Don't reuse the exact same story for two interviewers in the same loop** if you
   can help it — interviewers compare notes afterward, and a repeated story reads as
   a thin story bank.
6. **Bring your own questions.** Interviewers note whether candidates ask good
   questions back — it's read as genuine curiosity and engagement, not just
   politeness.

### The Bar Raiser

One interviewer in most Amazon loops is a **Bar Raiser** — someone from *outside*
the hiring team, specifically trained to hold the bar high and empowered to veto a
hire even if everyone else says yes. Their job is to check whether you'd perform
better than roughly half of the people already in the role, and they're known for
digging in hard on follow-ups, looking for inconsistency or vagueness. Treat every
LP story as if a Bar Raiser might be the one asking — specific numbers, specific
actions, no team-pronoun answers.

## 1. Customer Obsession

> "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards. They work vigorously to earn
> and keep customer trust. Although leaders pay attention to competitors, they
> obsess over customers."

**What it really means:** Decisions start from customer impact, not internal
convenience, not "what's easiest to build," not "what the competitor did."

**Signals interviewers look for:** you can name the actual customer (external user,
internal team, stakeholder) and tie your action directly to their outcome, backed by
data or direct feedback — not just an assumption of what they'd want.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you went above and beyond for a customer.
- Describe a time you had to balance customer needs against business or technical
  constraints.
- Tell me about a time you used customer feedback to change a decision or a
  roadmap.

**Tip:** Always name the customer explicitly and quantify the impact (reduced churn
by X%, cut support tickets by Y, saved Z hours/week) — "the customer was happy" is
not a result.

---

## 2. Ownership

> "Leaders are owners. They think long term and don't sacrifice long-term value for
> short-term results. They act on behalf of the entire company, beyond just their
> own team. They never say 'that's not my job.'"

**What it really means:** Act like an owner of the whole company, not a ticket
executor. Think in quarters/years, not sprints. Follow through past the point where
it stopped being "your job."

**Signals interviewers look for:** you took on responsibility outside your formal
scope, and — critically — you *finished* it, not just started it.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you took on something outside your job description.
- Describe a time you made a decision that hurt a short-term metric but was right
  for the long term.
- Tell me about a mistake you owned and personally fixed.

**Tip:** Ownership is proven at the finish line, not the start line — a story that
ends at "I raised the issue" is weaker than one that ends at "I drove it to
resolution, including the unglamorous cleanup afterward."

---

## 3. Invent and Simplify

> "Leaders expect and require innovation and invention from their teams and always
> find ways to simplify. They are externally aware, look for new ideas from
> everywhere, and are not limited by 'not invented here.' As we do new things, we
> accept that we may be misunderstood for long periods of time."

**What it really means:** Innovation *and* simplification are both explicitly named
— removing complexity counts as much as adding a clever new feature.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you simplified an actual process/system (fewer
steps, automated a manual task, cut unnecessary complexity), or pursued a non-obvious
idea while being okay with early skepticism.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about the most innovative thing you've built or proposed.
- Describe a time you simplified a complex process or system.
- Tell me about an idea you had that others were initially skeptical of.

**Tip:** "Invent" doesn't require a patent-worthy breakthrough — a good
simplification story (fewer manual steps, an automated pipeline replacing a
spreadsheet) is a completely legitimate answer here.

---

## 4. Are Right, A Lot

> "Leaders are right a lot. They have strong judgment and good instincts. They seek
> diverse perspectives and work to disconfirm their beliefs."

**What it really means:** Strong judgment paired with genuine intellectual honesty
— actively seeking out reasons you might be wrong, not just defending your first
instinct.

**Signals interviewers look for:** a case where your judgment paid off *and* a case
where you updated your view after new evidence — both count.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time your instinct or judgment was right despite pushback.
- Tell me about a time you were wrong — how did you find out, and what did you do
  about it?

**Tip:** This is the one LP where openly describing being wrong (and how you updated)
often scores *better* than insisting you're always right — the official text
literally says leaders "work to disconfirm their beliefs."

---

## 5. Learn and Be Curious

> "Leaders are never done learning and always seek to improve themselves. They are
> curious about new possibilities and act to explore them."

**What it really means:** Continuous, self-directed learning — and *acting* on
curiosity, not just having it.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you proactively learned something outside your
comfort zone and applied it to a concrete outcome.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you had to learn something completely new, quickly.
- Describe a skill you taught yourself specifically to solve a problem.

**Tip:** Tie the learning to a concrete outcome. "I learned X" on its own reads as a
hobby; "I learned X, which let me ship Y" reads as leadership.

---

## 6. Hire and Develop the Best

> "Leaders raise the performance bar with every hire and promotion. They recognize
> exceptional talent, and willingly move them throughout the organization. Leaders
> develop leaders and take seriously their role in coaching others. We work on
> behalf of our people to invent mechanisms for development like Career Choice."

**What it really means:** Raising the talent bar and actively developing others —
not just being good yourself.

**Signals interviewers look for:** mentoring, coaching, giving hard feedback,
raising a team's hiring or quality bar.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you mentored someone.
- Describe giving difficult feedback to a peer or a report.
- Tell me about a time you helped raise your team's hiring or quality bar.

**Tip:** If you've never formally managed anyone, this LP still applies — reframe
around mentoring an intern/junior teammate, or raising a code-review/design-review
standard for your team.

---

## 7. Insist on the Highest Standards

> "Leaders have relentlessly high standards — many people may think these standards
> are unreasonably high. Leaders are continually raising the bar and drive their
> teams to deliver high quality products, services, and processes. Leaders ensure
> that defects do not get sent down the line and that problems are fixed so they
> stay fixed."

**What it really means:** A refusal to let quality slide, even under time pressure —
and fixing root causes, not just symptoms.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you blocked a release, rejected substandard work,
or pushed back on cutting a corner, even when it was inconvenient to do so.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you rejected work that didn't meet your bar.
- Describe a time you raised the quality bar on a project despite pushback or time
  pressure.

**Tip:** This LP is in deliberate tension with *Deliver Results* — a strong answer
shows you know when to hold the line on quality and when shipping is the right call;
naming that tradeoff explicitly is a strong signal.

---

## 8. Think Big

> "Thinking small is a self-fulfilling prophecy. Leaders create and communicate a
> bold direction that inspires results. They think differently and look around
> corners for ways to serve customers."

**What it really means:** Reaching for a bolder solution than the one that was
literally asked for.

**Signals interviewers look for:** a clear contrast between "the small ask I got"
and "the bigger reframing I proposed" — the delta between the two is the story.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about your most ambitious idea or project.
- Describe a time you pushed for a bigger solution than what was originally asked
  for.

**Tip:** Explicitly state the original, smaller scope before describing your bigger
version — without that contrast, the interviewer can't tell you actually "thought
big" versus just did the assigned task well.

---

## 9. Bias for Action

> "Speed matters in business. Many decisions and actions are reversible and do not
> need extensive study. We value calculated risk taking."

**What it really means:** Speed over exhaustive analysis — specifically for
decisions that are reversible, and specifically framed as *calculated* risk, not
recklessness.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you moved with incomplete information because the
decision was cheap to reverse, and you can explain why it was safe to move fast.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you took a calculated risk.
- Describe a decision you made quickly with limited data.
- Tell me about a time you had to act without complete information.

**Tip:** Always name *why* the decision was reversible or low-risk-to-try — that
"calculated" framing is exactly what separates this from a recklessness story.

---

## 10. Frugality

> "Accomplish more with less. Constraints breed resourcefulness, self-sufficiency,
> and invention. There are no extra points for growing headcount, budget size, or
> fixed expense."

**What it really means:** Treat constraints as a feature that forces a better
solution, not an obstacle to complain about.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you solved a real problem using existing/cheap
resources instead of requesting more budget or headcount.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you accomplished something significant with limited
  resources.
- Describe solving a problem without adding headcount or budget.

**Tip:** Frame the constraint as what *forced* a more elegant solution — never frame
it as "we were under-resourced and it was hard," which reads as a complaint, not a
principle in action.

---

## 11. Earn Trust

> "Leaders listen attentively, speak candidly, and treat others respectfully. They
> are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing. Leaders
> do not believe their or their team's body odor smells of perfume. They benchmark
> themselves and their teams against the best."

**What it really means:** Candid, respectful communication — and explicit
self-criticism, even when uncomfortable.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you delivered hard or unwelcome feedback
respectfully, or — the stronger version — you publicly owned your *own* mistake
rather than deflecting it.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you had to give unwelcome feedback or deliver bad news.
- Describe a time you lost someone's trust and how you rebuilt it.
- Tell me about a time you were transparent about your own mistake.

**Tip:** "Vocally self-critical" is explicitly in the official text — a story where
*you* are the one exposing your own error (not someone else's) lands unusually well
for this LP specifically.

---

## 12. Dive Deep

> "Leaders operate at all levels, stay connected to the details, audit frequently,
> and are skeptical when metrics and anecdote differ. No task is beneath them."

**What it really means:** Going to the ground truth yourself — logs, raw data, the
actual query — instead of trusting a dashboard or a secondhand summary.

**Signals interviewers look for:** a specific, concrete detail that proves you were
genuinely in the weeds (an actual log line, a specific query, an exact number), not
a vague "I investigated the issue."

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you found a root cause that others had missed.
- Describe a time you dug into data that contradicted the reported metrics.

**Tip:** Include one specific, almost mundane detail (the exact field name, the
specific off-by-one, the specific log timestamp) — that concreteness is what proves
"dive deep" actually happened, versus being asserted.

---

## 13. Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit

> "Leaders are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree,
> even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting. Leaders have conviction and are
> tenacious. They do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion. Once a decision
> is determined, they commit wholly."

**What it really means:** Two halves, and both matter: push back with conviction
when you disagree, *then* commit fully once the decision is made — even if it didn't
go your way.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you challenged a senior person or a consensus
view with data or reasoning, **and** you followed through fully on the final
decision even if it wasn't the one you argued for.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or team and how it was
  resolved.
- Describe a time you had to commit to and execute a decision you didn't personally
  agree with.

**Tip:** The "commit" half is as important as the "disagree" half. A story that ends
at "I convinced them I was right" only covers half the LP — the stronger version
ends with you fully executing a decision that went *against* you.

---

## 14. Deliver Results

> "Leaders focus on the key inputs for their business and deliver them with the
> right quality and in a timely fashion. Despite setbacks, they rise to the
> occasion and never settle."

**What it really means:** Focus on the inputs that actually drive the outcome, and
push through setbacks rather than making excuses for them.

**Signals interviewers look for:** a hard deadline or goal hit despite real
obstacles, with a quantified outcome at the end.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you delivered results despite significant obstacles.
- Describe your most impactful project and its measurable outcome.
- Tell me about a time you had to leave a task unfinished (prioritization angle —
  what did you deliver instead, and why was that the right call?).

**Tip:** This is the "so what" LP — every answer should end on a number: a
percentage, a dollar figure, a time saved, a count of users or systems affected.

---

## 15. Strive to be Earth's Best Employer

> "Leaders work every day to create a safer, more productive, higher performing,
> more diverse, and more just work environment. They lead with empathy, have fun at
> work, and make it easy for others to have fun. Leaders ask themselves: Are my
> fellow employees growing? Are they empowered? Are they ready for what's next?
> Leaders have a vision for and commitment to their employees' personal success,
> whether that be at Amazon or elsewhere."

**What it really means:** Leadership that actively improves the environment and
growth of the people around you, not just the output of the team.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you improved team culture, morale, or
psychological safety, or actively advocated for a colleague's growth — even at the
cost of losing them to another team or company.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you improved team morale or culture.
- Describe a time you advocated for a teammate's growth or career, even if it
  didn't directly benefit you or your team.

**Tip:** This is one of the newer LPs (added 2021) — fewer generic "canned" answers
exist for it online, so an authentic, specific example here tends to stand out more
than for the older, more heavily-rehearsed principles.

---

## 16. Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility

> "We started in a garage, but we're not there anymore. We are big, we impact the
> world, and we are far from perfect. We must be humble and thoughtful about even
> the secondary effects of our actions. Our local communities, planet, and future
> generations need us to be better every day. We must begin each day with a
> determination to make better, do better, and be better for our customers, our
> employees, our partners, and the world at large. And we must end every day knowing
> we can do even more tomorrow. Leaders create more than they consume and always
> leave things better than how they found them."

**What it really means:** Humility about the broader, secondary consequences of
decisions made at scale — societal, environmental, community — not just the
immediate business metric.

**Signals interviewers look for:** you considered a downstream or external
consequence of a decision beyond your immediate team or sprint goal.

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you considered the broader impact of a decision beyond your
  immediate team.
- Describe a time you had to balance a business goal against a broader
  responsibility (accessibility, data privacy, environmental cost, community
  impact).

**Tip:** Also one of the newer LPs (added 2021, alongside #15) — good stories here
tend to involve accessibility, privacy, environmental cost, or community impact,
not just "we shipped it fast" (that's `Bias for Action`'s territory, not this one).

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer Obsession | Start from the customer, work backwards |
| 2 | Ownership | Act like a company owner; finish what you start |
| 3 | Invent and Simplify | Innovate *and* remove complexity |
| 4 | Are Right, A Lot | Strong judgment, actively seek to be proven wrong |
| 5 | Learn and Be Curious | Learn continuously, and act on it |
| 6 | Hire and Develop the Best | Raise the bar; coach others up |
